## 5.4 AI Agent Experiments

This section carries the thesis's primary contribution. It follows the agent's execution path:
classify (§5.4.1), validate (§5.4.2), execute and verbalise (§5.4.3), retrieve (§5.4.4), then
evaluates the composition end to end (§5.4.5) and against its cost (§5.4.6). Datasets, metric
definitions and the statistical protocol are settled in §5.2; the model configuration is given in
§5.1.1. Every experiment feeds the agent typed text rather than speech, so all figures are upper
bounds on what a customer speaking through the deployed pipeline would experience. §5.4.7 collects
the bounds on every result reported here.

Fourteen experiments follow. The table below names the one result that answers each objective from
§1.3; the rest is supporting detail.

### 5.4.1 Intent Classification and Routing

Objectives 1 and 2 require Vietnamese restaurant utterances to be classified into ordering, menu
search, payment or general conversation at 90 % or better, and require that accuracy to come from
a trained classifier rather than a language model, at a median latency an order of magnitude
lower. It is a joint claim and fails if either half fails: accuracy alone would not justify the
component, since a language model was already available, and latency alone would not either, since
a keyword matcher is faster still.

Five experiments follow. They ask whether accuracy holds on utterances that carry their meaning in
the text alone, whether it holds when the same words mean different things at different points in
a conversation, whether utterances carrying two intents are handled, whether the classifier
survives comparison against the approaches it replaces, and whether it learned the task rather
than the training distribution.

The classifier is the multi-layer perceptron of §4.5.2: a 768-dimensional sentence embedding
concatenated with ten conversation-state features, producing one of four intents with a confidence
score. It is trained on 434 hand-written spoken Vietnamese utterances expanded to 2,134 examples
by context augmentation, written against the reference menu rather than generated by a language
model.

#### Single-Intent Accuracy

On 149 hand-written cases balanced at roughly 37 per class, classifiable from the text alone, the
classifier is correct on 140, 94.0 %, Wilson 89.0–96.8 %. Median inference latency is 8.0 ms and
the 95th percentile 10.1 ms. Mean confidence is 0.969 on correct predictions and 0.961 overall.
Per class, F1 runs from 0.901 on CHAT through 0.914 on ORDER and 0.944 on SEARCH to 1.000 on
PAYMENT.

**Table 5.1.** Confusion matrix on the single-intent set. Rows are the true intent, columns the
predicted intent; diagonal entries are correct classifications.

| True \ Predicted | ORDER | SEARCH | PAYMENT | CHAT | Total |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **ORDER** | **37** | 0 | 0 | 1 | 38 |
| **SEARCH** | 2 | **34** | 0 | 1 | 37 |
| **PAYMENT** | 0 | 0 | **37** | 0 | 37 |
| **CHAT** | 4 | 1 | 0 | **32** | 37 |

The matrix matters more than the accuracy figure, because it shows which confusions occur. The
dangerous cell is empty: the entire PAYMENT row and column are clean, so no utterance was routed
into or out of billing. A misrouting between ordering and payment would either bill a customer who
did not ask or fail to bill one who did, and neither error is recoverable downstream.

All nine errors fall between ORDER, SEARCH and CHAT, and six are pulled toward ORDER, which is the
recoverable direction: an utterance misrouted into the order worker meets the validator, which
finds no resolvable dish name and either rejects the call or delegates to the chat worker. CHAT has
the lowest recall at 86.5 %, and four of its five errors contain the pronoun `tôi` followed by an
action verb resembling an ordering command, as in "Tôi muốn gọi thêm mà quên mất món đó tên gì rồi"
and "Tôi bị dị ứng hải sản thì gọi món gì được". Both contain `gọi` (to order) in a conversational
rather than transactional context, so the surface form genuinely supports both readings.

#### Context-Dependent Accuracy

The design claim behind the ten context features is that conversation state belongs inside the
feature vector rather than in a rule layer around it. The test is whether identical utterances
route differently depending on the order stage, over 70 cases formed from 36 utterance groups,
each evaluated at two stages.

With the context features the classifier is correct on 49 of 70, 70.0 %; with them replaced by
IDLE defaults it falls to 41 of 70, 58.6 %. The features corrected 11 cases and broke 3, giving
b = 11, c = 3 and p = 0.057. The improvement of 11.4 percentage points does not reach significance.

The gains are concentrated on short affirmations. "ok", "ừ", "đúng rồi", "được", "ok em", "Uh đúng
rồi đó" and "chuẩn" all route to ORDER when the order stage is AWAITING_CONFIRMATION and to CHAT
when it is IDLE, which is the intended behaviour. Context broke three cases, "chưa muốn đâu", "còn
gì nữa không" and "hết chưa", all routed to SEARCH or ORDER instead of CHAT. The 18 cases wrong in
both modes are dominated by ambiguous action verbs such as `thêm`, `lấy`, `cho`, `bỏ` and `đặt` at
an empty cart, where the classifier follows the surface ordering language and the label expects
CHAT on the grounds that there is nothing to act upon. These are genuinely underspecified.

#### Multi-Intent Detection

The classifier produces a single label, so an utterance carrying two intents such as "Cho 2 Ốc
Hương rồi tính tiền luôn" cannot be scored as a classification success or failure without a
category error. The architecture detects such utterances and routes them to the rewriter for
decomposition, so the metric is whether the router flags the utterance, not which label it assigns.

Detection fires on a boundary marker (`rồi`, `và`, `thì`, `xong`, `rồi thì`, `với lại`, `à mà`,
`,mà`) or on confidence below 0.7. On 27 genuinely multi-intent utterances, 23 are detected,
85.2 %: twenty-one by boundary markers, five by low confidence, three by both.

The four undetected cases contain no lexical boundary marker, their clauses joined by a comma or
fused without a connector, as in "Chốt đơn với bill luôn đi em" and "Cảm ơn em, cho anh xin bill
luôn nha". This is an inherent limit of keyword-based segmentation, and the failure is graceful:
the utterance routes to its dominant intent and the weaker one is absorbed, so the customer
receives a partial response rather than a wrong action. Three pseudo-multi-intent controls produced
two false alarms, both harmless, since the rewriter splits the utterance into two fragments of the
same intent and the result is correct at the cost of one extra rewriter call. No single-intent
utterance was routed to the wrong worker.

detection cases, the 39-case holdout, and 16 further cases carried over from earlier evaluation
files. Accuracy on it is lower than on the single-intent set alone, 86.2 % against 94.0 %, because
the context-dependent cases are deliberately hard and the carried-over ones probe failure modes
rather than sample typical usage.

A 39-case holdout was partitioned before context augmentation and never used for training or
tuning. The classifier is correct on 38 of 39, Wilson 86.8–99.5 %. Without context features the
figure is 36 of 39.

The single off-diagonal entry is again in the SEARCH-to-ORDER direction and again nothing touches

**Objectives 1 and 2 are met.** The classifier clears 90 % on held-out single-intent utterances and
repeats the result on a set partitioned before augmentation, it is significantly more accurate than
every routing approach the system previously used, it matches the deployed language model at a
twenty-fifth of the median cost, and its errors fall away from payment and toward the worker whose
validator refuses an unresolvable name.

*Limitation.* The context-feature ablation, which is what distinguishes this classifier from an
ordinary intent model, gains 11.4 points but does not reach significance (p = 0.057), so that half
of the design claim is reported rather than established. §5.6.4 states the bounds in full.

### 5.4.2 Action Validation and Safety

Objective 3 requires that no item absent from the menu reach the customer's cart. The requirement
has two sides and fails on either: a dish the kitchen cannot cook reaching the cart, or a gate
strict enough to refuse valid orders, since a validator that rejects everything satisfies the first
half perfectly. The validator of §4.5.4 is the component that lets a language model drive
restaurant operations without being trusted to generate correct arguments: the model proposes tool
calls, and deterministic Python checks every argument against the menu before any tool executes.

Four experiments follow. Tables 5.4 and 5.5 measure the gate's own decisions, Table 5.6 measures
what changes when it is removed, Table 5.7 tests it against input written to defeat it, and Table
5.8 measures the escape hatch a worker uses when no tool fits.

#### Name Resolution, Suggestion and Ambiguity

Resolution decides whether a spoken name is on the menu: both sides are normalised, then compared
for an exact match, a prefix, then a substring, and a name matching nothing is rejected. Suggestion
runs only after resolution has rejected a name, scoring token overlap by Jaccard similarity to
propose a near neighbour, and cannot admit an item to the cart. Both are deterministic, so the
figures are exact fractions.

**Table 5.4.** Name resolution and suggestion by stage (n = 70 cases).

| Stage | Correct | Total | Accuracy |
|-------|:-------:|:-----:|:--------:|

| Diacritic-insensitive | 10 | 10 | 100 % |
| Prefix match | 10 | 10 | 100 % |
| Substring match | 10 | 10 | 100 % |
| Misspelled, correctly rejected | 16 | 16 | 100 % |
| Suggestion offered (Jaccard ≥ 0.3) | 5 | 5 | 100 % |
| Suggestion withheld (Jaccard < 0.3) | 4 | 4 | 100 % |

The two rejection rows of Table 5.4 carry the result, not the diagonal. All 16 misspellings are

Table 5.5 tests the case where a name is valid but underspecified. "Ốc Hương" is a prefix of eleven
sauce variants on the reference menu, and the validator flags all 15 such names for clarification
instead of silently resolving to one of them, with no cost to the 10 unambiguous names, which
resolve directly. Appendix G.3 shows a live turn: the clarification the customer hears and the
follow-up turn that resolves it.

#### Validator Ablation

The validator node is replaced by a pass-through before the graph is constructed, and 41 scenarios
run through both configurations. Off-menu leakage is measured by resolving each item name appearing
in `add_cart` and `confirm_order` calls against the menu file directly rather than by reading the
validator's own validity flag, because the ablated arm has no validator to set that flag and a
measurement based on it would report zero by construction.

**Table 5.6.** Validator ablation (n = 41 scenarios per arm).

| Condition | Scenario pass rate | Off-menu items reaching cart tools | Bad `confirm_order` calls |
|-----------|:------------------:|:----------------------------------:|:-----------------------:|
| Validator ON | 92.7 % (38/41) | **0** | 1 |
| Validator OFF | 92.7 % (38/41) | **31** | **8** |

The 31 leaked names originate in 14 distinct scenarios. In a deployed restaurant they would be
dishes the kitchen cannot cook appearing on a customer's bill.

The identical pass rate is a null result worth stating plainly. The turn-level assertions check
tool selection and conversational flow, which the validator does not affect; what it changes is the
content of the arguments, which is what the leakage column measures and the pass rate does not. The
validator is a guarantee, not a correction the system visibly depends on to complete tasks.

#### Out-of-Menu Robustness

Thirty adversarial scenarios across seven categories test the rejection path: non-existent dishes,
near-miss variants of real dishes, mixed orders where one item of several is invalid, hallucination
bait such as an invented combo quoted at a specific price, inputs in teencode, inputs without
diacritics, and a negative control of entirely valid items that must not be rejected.

The workers run under `tool_choice="any"`, so the model must emit a tool call on every turn. Without
an escape, a worker receiving an utterance none of its tools fit would be forced to invent one. The

**Objective 3 is met.** No off-menu item reached a cart tool in any validated run against 31 with
the validator bypassed, and the gate achieved that without refusing valid work: the negative
control passed, all 15 ambiguous names were flagged rather than resolved silently, and none of the
16 misspellings was force-matched. Both sides of the requirement hold.

*Limitation.* The guarantee covers menu membership, not referential correctness, and protects
state, not speech: a `remove_cart` naming a genuine menu item the customer never ordered passes
through, which is the failure §5.4.5 exhibits, and the single bad `confirm_order` in the validated
arm was caught before the ledger while the response node still verbalised a confirmation. Two of
the thirty adversarial scenarios were false negatives. §5.6.4 states the bounds in full.

### 5.4.3 Multi-Intent Execution and Verbalisation

No numbered objective covers this property. It is reported because the experiments identified it as
the system's weakest point. Executing every intent in a turn is necessary but not sufficient: if the
agent adds two dishes and requests the bill but tells the customer about only one of the three
actions, the state is correct and the customer's understanding of it is not. Execution and
verbalisation are therefore counted separately. The dataset is 25 multi-intent turns with
per-intent lexical evidence, run five times because the response layer is stochastic.

**Table 5.9.** Multi-intent execution and verbalisation (25 turns, mean over 5 runs, observed range
in brackets).

| Measure | Result |
|---------|--------|
| Verbalisation rate | 0.725 [0.667–0.767] |
| Turns fully verbalised | **0.576** [0.520–0.640] |
| Coverage of what the customer asked for | 0.747 [0.700–0.780] |
| Turns where the router queued every expected intent | 0.600 (identical in all 5 runs) |
| Verbalisation rate, successful outcomes only | 0.625 [0.567–0.667] |
| Turns fully verbalised, successful outcomes only | 0.456 [0.400–0.520] |

The router figure is identical across all five runs, as expected for a deterministic classifier, so
every point of variance in this table originates in the language model rather than in routing.

The last two rows apply a stricter scoring rule and are reported because the difference between the
two rules, roughly twelve points of verbalisation rate and sixteen of fully-verbalised turns, is
larger than most effects in this chapter and belongs to the measurement rather than to the system.
The adopted rule counts an intent as verbalised when the customer learns its fate, success or
correct refusal alike; the stricter rule counts only successful outcomes. Case MI-011 shows why the
stricter rule is wrong: "Thêm 1 Bia Sài Gòn nữa và cho xin mã QR thanh toán" executes both intents
and answers both, the payment half with "hiện chưa có đơn hàng nào trong phiên này ạ", which
contains none of that intent's evidence terms (`qr`, `mã`, `thanh toán`, `chuyển khoản`) and was
therefore scored as never verbalised despite the customer having been told exactly what happened.
The refusal phrasings used by the adopted rule are taken from the agent's own response templates
and applied uniformly per intent; the generic string "có lỗi khi xử lý đơn" is excluded because it
does not identify which request went wrong. Re-scoring 125 stored replies under both rules
reproduces gaps of the same size, so the difference comes from the rule and not from sampling.

#### Where the Loss Falls

Across all five runs, 53 turns carry at least one executed but unspoken intent, and they are not
evenly distributed.

**Table 5.10.** Residual verbalisation losses by pattern (53 losses across 5 runs).

| Pattern | Losses | Which intent is lost |
|---------|:------:|----------------------|
| ORDER and SEARCH in the same turn | 26 | the search result |
| ORDER and PAYMENT in the same turn | 15 | the order acknowledgement |
| Router misrouted a single-intent turn | 7 | not a response-layer failure |
| Other | 5 | |

Forty-one of the 53 losses, roughly 77 %, occur when an ordering action is combined with a second
intent, and which of the two is lost depends on the pairing. This is a diagnosable defect rather
than a general tendency to drop intents.

What the cause is not is worth recording. The response pipeline aggregates correctly: the outcome
node returns every tool message produced since the last customer utterance and builds one response
context per tool, and the response node iterates that list and joins all of the resulting replies.
Scenario QS-003 in §5.4.5 shows a two-intent turn verbalising both halves. The loss is therefore
not a failure to aggregate contexts but a failure occurring earlier, in which certain intent
pairings do not produce the second context at all.

**This property is not met**, and §5.6.1 records it as such. On multi-intent turns the system
executes more than it says: it tells the customer the complete story on 57.6 % of such turns and
conveys 74.7 % of what was asked overall. What makes the result usable rather than merely
disappointing is its shape. The loss is a specific pairing defect rather than a general one, it
originates upstream of the response layer, and roughly a seventh of it belongs to the router rather
than to verbalisation at all.

*Limitation.* The diagnosis identifies where the second response context fails to appear without
identifying the code path responsible, so this is a located defect rather than a solved one.
§5.6.4 states the bounds in full.

### 5.4.4 Knowledge Retrieval

Objective 4 requires the system to retrieve relevant dishes from the 219-entry menu in response to
Vietnamese sensory descriptions rather than dish names, measured by recall and mean reciprocal rank
at rank five. The requirement has a negative half: when no dish matches, the pipeline must return
nothing rather than fabricate. Recall and hit rate carry more weight than precision, because the
agent paraphrases what it retrieves instead of presenting a ranked list, so it can ignore a weak
result but cannot recover a dish the retriever never returned.

Three failure modes are measured separately. Fusion may fail to improve on the two indices it
combines, in which case the second index costs latency for nothing. Quality may hold on queries
phrased in menu vocabulary and collapse on the sensory phrasing the objective targets. And the gate
may admit queries no dish answers. The pipeline is described in §4.6: a lexical index over dish
names and descriptions, a dense vector index over the same corpus, a fusion of the two rankings,
and a gate that inspects the result before any of it reaches the language model. The dataset is 24
menu queries with graded relevance judgements across three difficulty levels.

#### Retrieval Quality and the Fusion Ablation

**Table 5.11.** Retrieval quality by mode (n = 24 queries).

| Mode | P@5 | R@5 | MRR | Hit Rate | p50 | p95 |
|------|:----:|:----:|:----:|:--------:|:----:|:----:|
| BM25 only | 0.367 | 0.719 | 0.720 | 0.875 | 0.5 ms | 0.8 ms |
| FAISS only | 0.315 | 0.523 | 0.663 | 0.792 | 6.9 ms | 8.9 ms |
| **RRF fusion** | **0.400** | **0.743** | **0.751** | **0.917** | 8.9 ms | 10.9 ms |

Reciprocal rank fusion improves on both constituent retrievers on all four quality metrics at
once, which is the result the architecture requires. Fusion that improved precision at the cost of
recall, or ranking at the cost of coverage, would present a trade-off to justify; this does not. A
hit rate of 0.917 means the retriever finds at least one relevant dish for 22 of 24 queries, and a
recall of 0.743 means it finds most of what could have answered each one.

The lexical index outperforms the dense index on every metric, which is expected for a menu corpus:
customers name dishes using the words printed on the menu, so lexical overlap is high and the
vocabulary gap dense retrieval exists to bridge is narrow. The dense index earns its place by
covering the queries BM25 misses rather than by outperforming it on average, which is what fusion
exploits. It costs about 7 ms against the lexical lane's half millisecond, almost all of it spent
encoding the query, so the semantic lane is roughly fourteen times more expensive in relative terms
and negligible in absolute ones: 8.9 ms against the median turn of 2.15 s in §5.4.6 is four tenths
of one percent of the turn budget. These figures are medians with the index and the embedding model
already resident; a first query against a cold process pays a model load of roughly ten seconds,
which the deployment avoids by loading both at startup.

**Table 5.12.** Fused retrieval quality by query difficulty.

| Difficulty | n | P@5 | R@5 | MRR | Hit Rate |
|------------|:--:|:----:|:----:|:----:|:--------:|
| Easy | 8 | 0.425 | 0.865 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| Medium | 9 | 0.444 | 0.907 | 0.722 | 1.000 |
| Hard | 7 | 0.314 | 0.391 | 0.505 | 0.714 |

The breakdown locates the failure. On easy and medium queries the retriever finds a relevant dish
for every query, and on easy queries the first result is always relevant. Performance collapses on
hard queries: recall falls from 0.907 to 0.391 and hit rate from 1.000 to 0.714, meaning two of the
seven return nothing relevant at all. The hard queries describe an information need through terms
absent from the menu text, such as asking for something suitable for a group to share, where no
dish description contains the concept of sharing. This is the structural retrieval failure
identified in §2.5.1: the query and the documents occupy disconnected regions of the vocabulary,
and no amount of index tuning closes a gap that exists in the corpus rather than in the retriever.

#### Dual-Lane Gatekeeper

The gate admits a query's results only if at least one lane provides evidence of relevance: the
semantic lane requires the top result's cosine similarity to exceed a threshold, the lexical lane
requires a keyword match in the top-ranked document. When neither passes, the pipeline returns
empty results rather than passing weakly related documents to the language model.

**Table 5.13.** Gatekeeper outcomes (n = 24 queries).

| Outcome | Count |
|---------|:-----:|
| Both lanes pass | 20 |
| Lexical lane only | 3 |
| Semantic lane only | 0 |
| Correctly rejected | 1 |
| **Total admitted** | **23 (95.8 %)** |

Top-1 cosine similarities range from 0.165 to 0.581, all within the valid interval. The single
rejection is "có gì cho nhóm đông người chia sẻ", where the semantic lane scores 0.195 and the
lexical lane finds no keyword match. This is a correct rejection: no dish description addresses
group sharing, so the alternative is passing irrelevant dishes to the language model and inviting
it to justify them.

The semantic lane never admits a query on its own. On 20 of 24 queries both lanes agree and on the
remaining three the lexical lane admits alone, at cosine similarities between 0.165 and 0.278. On a
menu corpus this is expected, since customers use menu vocabulary and the lexical lane is
consequently strong nearly everywhere. The semantic lane's contribution is to the ranking through
fusion rather than to the admission decision, so on this corpus the gate is effectively lexical.

**Objective 4 is met.** Fusion outperforms both indices it combines on all four quality metrics at
once, so the second index buys coverage without presenting a trade-off to justify, and the gate
rejected only the one query the corpus genuinely cannot answer, which satisfies the requirement
that the pipeline return nothing rather than fabricate.

*Limitation.* The collapse on hard queries is a ceiling on this corpus rather than on this
retriever, since no index over documents that never mention sharing can answer a question about
sharing. §5.6.4 states the bounds in full.

### 5.4.5 End-to-End System Evaluation

Objective 6 requires the system to complete full ordering scenarios, from a customer request
through a confirmed order to the kitchen display, across conversations that include ambiguous dish
names, off-menu items and changes of mind. The session lifecycle of seating, orders, payment and
table release must be enforced across all components while those scenarios run, and the cart must
stay consistent throughout. Component-level accuracy establishes neither: a router that classifies
correctly, a validator that blocks hallucinations and a retriever that finds the right dishes can
still combine into a system that fails to complete an order.

Seven conversations were authored and six are reported, the seventh being a modification scenario
subsumed by the twelve-turn sitting. Each is presented as it actually ran: the customer's
Vietnamese utterance, the tool calls produced, the order stage committed to state, and the reply
spoken back. This section reports conversations rather than a pass rate, because a pass rate over
six scenarios would carry an interval too wide to support any claim and would discard the visible
sequence of what the system did and said.

**Table 5.14.** The six conversations, the claim each exercises, the outcome, and where the
transcript appears.

| Conversation | What it exercises | Outcome | Transcript |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordering, confirming, paying | The baseline path, with colloquial phrasings for closing an order and asking to be billed | Complete; billed total equals cart total | Appendix G.1 |
| Referring back to a search result | Resolving "món đó" against the previous turn's search | Complete; the full dish name reaches `add_cart` | Appendix G.2 |
| Two intents in one utterance | Boundary-marker detection and rewriter decomposition | Complete; fragments execute in the order spoken | below, first turn |

Transcripts are verbatim, with the customer's turns marked `[Khách]` and the agent's `[AI]`.

#### Two Intents in One Utterance

```
[Khách]     Cho 2 Hàu Nướng Phô Mai rồi tính tiền luôn nha em
[Tools]     add_cart(Hàu Nướng Phô Mai ×2)
[Tools]     request_payment(table_id=T_qs_3)
[Stage]     AWAITING_CONFIRMATION, cart 78.000₫
[AI]        Dạ, giỏ hàng của anh/chị hiện có:
            - Hàu Nướng Phô Mai ×2 (39.000₫/phần)
            Tổng tạm tính 78.000. Anh/chị xác nhận đặt hàng chưa ạ?
            Dạ, hiện chưa có đơn hàng nào trong phiên này ạ.
```

The boundary marker `rồi` triggers the rewriter, which splits the utterance into single-intent
fragments that the classifier then labels independently. This is the central claim of the routing
design in operation: a classifier trained only on single-intent utterances handles multi-intent
speech because decomposition happens before classification rather than inside it. The second turn,
"Xác nhận đơn rồi bill luôn", is decomposed the same way and produces `confirm_order` followed by
`request_payment`, with both outcomes verbalised.

The first turn also shows the system declining to be clever. The fragments execute in the order
spoken, so payment is attempted before the order is confirmed, and the agent answers truthfully
that no order exists rather than inventing a total or silently reordering the request. The reply is
awkward but honest, and guessing what the customer meant is what the architecture is built to avoid.

#### A Full Sitting

The longest conversation follows a group of first-time customers through twelve turns: browsing,
ordering, asking for more recommendations, two substitutions, confirmation and payment.

```
 1 [Khách]  Quán mình có món nào bán chạy nhất không em, tụi mình lần đầu tới quán
   [Tools]  search(query="món bán chạy, best seller")
   [AI]     Dạ, anh/chị có muốn thử món tôm thẻ nướng muối ớt không? Đây là một
            trong những best seller của chúng em ạ.

 2 [Khách]  Ốc Hương Xốt Trứng Muối giá bao nhiêu vậy em
   [Tools]  delegate(reason="khách hỏi về giá món ăn, không phải yêu cầu CRUD")
   [AI]     Dạ, Ốc Hương Xốt Trứng Muối là một best seller của chúng em,
            giá là 85.000₫ ạ. Anh/chị có muốn thử món này không?

 3 [Khách]  Ok cho 2 phần Ốc Hương Xốt Trứng Muối với 4 Bia Sài Gòn nha
   [Tools]  add_cart(Ốc Hương Xốt Trứng Muối ×2, Bia Sài Gòn ×4)
   [Stage]  AWAITING_CONFIRMATION, cart 230.000₫

 4 [Khách]  Có món nào ăn nhậu lai rai không em, gợi ý thêm vài món đi
   [Tools]  search(query="món nhậu, đồ nướng")
   [AI]     Dạ, chị/chị có thể thử Sò Điệp Nướng Phô Mai, món này rất ngon ạ.

 5 [Khách]  Vậy cho thêm 1 Mực Cháy Tỏi với 1 Khoai Tây Lắc Phô Mai nữa
   [Tools]  add_cart(Mực Cháy Tỏi ×1, Khoai Tây Lắc Phô Mai ×1)
   [Stage]  AWAITING_CONFIRMATION, cart 414.000₫

 6 [Khách]  Cho mình xem lại giỏ hàng đang có gì
   [Tools]  delegate(reason="khách hỏi xem lại giỏ hàng, không phải yêu cầu CRUD")
   [AI]     (reads back all four lines with the running total)

 7 [Khách]  Thôi bỏ Bia Sài Gòn đi, đổi qua 4 Bia Heineken
   [Tools]  remove_cart("Bia Sài Gòn"), add_cart(Bia Heineken ×4)
   [Stage]  AWAITING_CONFIRMATION, cart 454.000₫

 8 [Khách]  Mực Cháy Tỏi ăn có cay không em? Tụi mình có đứa không ăn cay được
   [Tools]  search(query="Mực Cháy Tỏi, cay")
   [AI]     Dạ chị/chị có muốn thử ốc bulot cháy tỏi ạ? Món này rất ngon và
            cay vừa miệng, phù hợp với khẩu vị của chị/chị.

 9 [Khách]  Thôi đổi Mực Cháy Tỏi qua Cháo Hàu đi, ăn Cháo Hàu cho lành
   [Tools]  remove_cart("Mực Cháy Tỏi"), add_cart(Cháo Hàu ×1)
   [Stage]  AWAITING_CONFIRMATION, cart 414.000₫

10 [Khách]  Tổng cộng hết bao nhiêu rồi em
   [Tools]  request_payment(table_id=T_qs_6)
   [AI]     Dạ, hiện chưa có đơn hàng nào trong phiên này ạ.

11 [Khách]  Ok chốt đơn đi em, đúng hết rồi đó
   [Tools]  confirm_order(items=[Ốc Hương Xốt Trứng Muối ×2,
            Khoai Tây Lắc Phô Mai ×1, Bia Heineken ×4, Cháo Hàu ×1])
   [Stage]  CONFIRMED

12 [Khách]  Tính tiền giùm tụi mình luôn nha em
   [Tools]  request_payment(table_id=T_qs_6)
   [AI]     Dạ, tổng hóa đơn của anh/chị là 414.000₫ ạ.
            Anh/chị vui lòng quét mã QR để thanh toán nhé.
```

State integrity holds across the whole sitting. The order stage remains at AWAITING_CONFIRMATION
from turn 3 through turn 10 while the cart is mutated four times, then advances on confirmation.
Both substitutions execute correctly and the confirmed order carries exactly the four items the
group settled on, with neither the Bia Sài Gòn removed on turn 7 nor the Mực Cháy Tỏi removed on
turn 9 appearing in it. The addition on turn 5 adds to the cart rather than replacing it. Cart
arithmetic is performed in Python throughout: the final bill of 414.000₫ is the sum of 170.000,
100.000, 49.000 and 95.000, recomputed from the menu price map after each mutation, which is why
the running totals are correct at every step rather than approximately correct.

Turns 2 and 6 show the delegate mechanism doing useful work. Neither a price question nor a request
to review the cart is a create-or-modify operation, and a worker obliged to call a tool would
otherwise have to invent one. Both are handed to the chat worker, and the price of 85.000₫ on turn
2 is correct and comes from curated memory rather than a retrieval call.

Two turns fall short. On turn 8 the customer asks whether Mực Cháy Tỏi is spicy. The retriever
returns that dish as its top lexical hit, carrying the taste attribute "cay thơm", and the rewriter,
presented with several dishes whose names contain "cháy tỏi", answers about a different one. The
retrieval layer supplies the right data and the language model fails to discriminate among similarly
named dishes. This reproduced in every run of the conversation, so it is a stable limitation rather
than sampling variance, and it is the clearest evidence in this chapter for the model-capability
boundary discussed in §6.2.

On turn 10 the customer asks what the cart comes to so far. The utterance routes to PAYMENT, the
payment tool queries the order ledger, finds no confirmed order and answers truthfully that none
exists. No wrong figure is produced, but the answer the customer wanted was 414.000₫ and it was
sitting in cart state at that moment. The failure is one of intent granularity: the four-class
scheme has no label distinguishing a request for the running cart total from a request to be
billed, and both surface forms are close enough in Vietnamese that a single PAYMENT class absorbs
them.

#### Dishes That Do Not Exist

The final conversation is adversarial. The customer orders two dishes that are not on the menu
alongside one that is, then refers vaguely to "those other ones".

```
[Khách]  1  Cho mình 1 tô Phở Bò Tái, 2 Ốc Hương Xốt Me với 1 dĩa Cơm Tấm Sườn
   [Tools] add_cart(Ốc Hương Xốt Me ×2)
   [Stage] AWAITING_CONFIRMATION, cart 170.000₫
   [AI]    Dạ, món Phở Bò Tái, Cơm Tấm Sườn hiện không có trong thực đơn ạ.
           Anh/chị muốn chọn món khác không ạ?

[Khách]  2  Vậy thôi cho mình Ốc Hương Xốt Me đi, mấy món kia bỏ
   [Tools] remove_cart("Cơm Tấm Sườn"), remove_cart("Lẩu Thái"),
           remove_cart("Bia Sài Gòn"), remove_cart("Trà Đào Cam Sả"),
           add_cart(Ốc Hương Xốt Me ×1), remove_cart("Ốc Hương Xốt Me")
   [Stage] IDLE, cart empty
   [AI]    Dạ, xin lỗi anh/chị, có lỗi khi xử lý đơn. Anh/chị thử lại
           giúp em nhé ạ. (repeated three times)

[Khách]  3  Chốt đơn đi
   [Tools] clear_cart()
   [Stage] IDLE, is_valid = false
```

The first turn is the validator working as designed and is the single most important turn in this
section. Phở Bò Tái and Cơm Tấm Sườn are plausible Vietnamese dishes this seafood restaurant does
not serve. The language model proposed all three items; the validator resolved each name against
the menu, rejected two and allowed one. The reply names both rejected dishes explicitly and offers
no substitute, and the cart contains one item at the correct price. Without this check, two dishes
the kitchen cannot cook would be on the bill.

The second turn is where the design reaches its boundary. "Mấy món kia bỏ" has no resolvable
antecedent, because the cart holds a single item and the dishes the customer is thinking of were
never added to it. Obliged by forced tool calling to produce something, the order worker emits six
calls: four removals naming dishes the customer never mentioned, of which Lẩu Thái, Bia Sài Gòn and
Trà Đào Cam Sả are hallucinated outright, followed by an addition and an immediate removal of the
only item actually present. The cart goes from correct to empty and the customer receives three
stacked apologies.

This scopes the validation claim precisely. The validator resolves every dish name against the
menu, which is why the invalid names in turn 1 are blocked. It does not check whether a named item
is present in the cart, because a `remove_cart` call naming a genuine menu item is not a
menu-validity error. The guarantee is over menu membership, not referential correctness. Delegation
does not rescue the turn either, since it exists for the case where the model recognises that no
tool applies, and here the model believed a tool did apply and supplied wrong arguments.

**Objective 6 is partially met.** Four of the six conversations complete correctly end to end,
including two built to break the system, and in all six the deterministic layer holds: the
validator, the name resolver, the cart arithmetic and the state machine behave correctly
throughout, and every conversation reaching payment bills exactly what the cart contained. The
lifecycle requirement holds without exception across a twelve-turn sitting with four cart mutations
and two substitutions. What fails in the other two is the language model's judgement, in two
specific situations: choosing among dishes whose names differ by one or two words, and acting on a
referring expression with no antecedent. Both are cases where the model resolves meaning rather
than selects a tool, and both are the kind of failure the validation layer is positioned to contain
but not to prevent.

*Limitation.* Six conversations support a pattern rather than a rate, and each is a single run of a
stochastic system. §5.6.4 states the bounds in full.

### 5.4.6 Agent Latency and Cost

Objective 5 requires a reply within five seconds at the median, measured from the arrival of a
transcript to the completion of the reply text. Chapter 4 sets the same budget over a wider span,
from the end of the customer's speech to the start of the reply, which adds voice activity
detection, transcription and speech synthesis on top of everything measured here. The two are
therefore not the same quantity, and the distance between them is the part of the budget this
chapter does not measure.

Twelve utterances spanning the intent classes were each executed five times, giving 60
measurements, broken down by intent class because the classes exercise different paths and a
pooled figure would hide the heaviest.

**Table 5.15.** Turn latency by intent class.

| Intent | p50 | p95 | Measurements |
|--------|:-----:|:-----:|:--:|
| ORDER | 1.36 s | 2.29 s | 10 |
| ORDER_CONFIRM | 3.22 s | 4.89 s | 5 |
| SEARCH | 2.19 s | 2.86 s | 15 |
| PAYMENT | 0.94 s | 3.66 s | 10 |
| CHAT | 1.22 s | 2.83 s | 10 |
| Multi-intent | 2.36 s | 4.21 s | 10 |
| **All turns** | **2.15 s** | **3.40 s** | **60** |

Median turn latency is 2.15 s and the 95th percentile 3.40 s, both inside the five-second budget of
§4.1. The heaviest path is order confirmation at a 95th percentile of 4.89 s, driven by
`confirm_order` performing a database write and a kitchen display push in addition to the language
model call. Multi-intent turns are second heaviest, as expected, since they invoke the rewriter and
then execute two workers in sequence.

Two points about the per-arm routing latencies in Table 5.2 bear on deployment. The 220 ms the
language-model arm adds over the classifier is not decisive against a five-second budget on its
own, yet it is spent before any useful work begins and on every turn, including the turns the
classifier resolves with near-perfect accuracy. And the previous hybrid router shows why a median
alone is insufficient: at 12 ms its median is close to the classifier's, because the semantic stage
resolves most queries without escalating, while its 95th percentile of 705 ms falls on the queries
that do escalate and pay the full small-model cost on top of the semantic stage already spent. One
turn in twenty was roughly seventy times slower than typical, which a customer notices as the
system occasionally hanging.

**Objective 5 is met.** Median turn latency is 2.15 s and the 95th percentile 3.40 s, both inside
the budget, and even the heaviest class stays under it at the 95th percentile. The Chapter 4 budget
is met for the portion measured; the speech stages separating the two definitions remain
unmeasured, so the roughly 2.8 s of headroom at the median is what the voice pipeline has to fit
into rather than a claim that it does.

*Limitation.* The percentiles rest on twelve distinct utterances repeated five times rather than
sixty different ones, and no per-node breakdown was instrumented, so the figures establish that the
budget is met without establishing which stage consumes it. §5.6.4 states the bounds in full.
